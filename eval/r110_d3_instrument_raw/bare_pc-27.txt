The central hypothesis is that the Biginelli reaction proceeds through a reversible initial condensation of the aldehyde and urea to form an intermediate iminium species or ureido-aldimine, which then undergoes a Michael addition with the $\beta$-keto ester. The rate-determining step often involves the elimination of water to form the final dihydropyrimidinone ring. To eliminate hazardous solvents while maintaining yield, one must exploit the thermodynamic stability of the product and the kinetic accessibility of the pathway under solvent-free or aqueous conditions.

The mechanism can be exploited for green synthesis by shifting the reaction medium to water or solid-phase catalysts that operate effectively in the absence of organic solvents. Water acts not just as a solvent but can facilitate the reaction through specific effects such as the hydrophobic effect, which concentrates the organic reactants into the aqueous phase, increasing their effective molarity and accelerating the condensation steps. Alternatively, using heterogeneous solid-supported acids or metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) allows the reaction to proceed in solvent-free conditions or in benign solvents like water or ethanol. These solid catalysts provide specific acid sites that activate the carbonyl groups of the aldehyde and $\beta$-keto ester, lowering the activation energy for the C-C bond formation and subsequent cyclization.

By removing hazardous solvents, the process becomes safer and easier to scale, as solvent disposal is eliminated. The key to maintaining high yield lies in ensuring efficient mass transfer and catalytic turnover. Under microwave irradiation in solvent-free conditions, the energy input is rapid and uniform, driving off the byproduct water and shifting the equilibrium toward the product according to Le Chatelier’s principle. Similarly, in aqueous media, the insolubility of the organic product facilitates easy separation, driving the reaction to completion. The exploitation of these mechanisms demonstrates that the inherent reactivity of the Biginelli components is robust enough to tolerate benign conditions, provided that the catalytic environment is optimized to stabilize the transition states for the key bond-forming steps. This approach confirms that sustainable protocols can achieve yields comparable to traditional toxic methods by leveraging water’s unique properties or the localized acidity of solid catalysts.
