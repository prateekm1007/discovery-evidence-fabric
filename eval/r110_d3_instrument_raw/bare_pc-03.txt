The most cost-effective synthesis pathway exploits the high thermal stability of the aromatic ring in terephthalic acid recovered from PET, leveraging a cascade of electrophilic aromatic substitution and Hofmann rearrangement chemistry. The hypothesis is that direct nitration of terephthalic acid is kinetically unfavorable due to the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the carboxyl groups, so the mechanism must be circumvented by temporarily activating the ring through esterification or, more effectively, by synthesizing terephthalonitrile as an intermediate, though the prompt's reference to Hofmann rearrangement suggests a route via amides is the intended focus.

Here is the proposed multi-step sequence:

1.  **PET Hydrolysis:** Perform alkaline hydrolysis (saponification) of PET waste using NaOH at elevated temperatures to yield sodium terephthalate, which is then acidified to precipitate terephthalic acid (TPA). This step is well-established and cost-effective.
2.  **Conversion to Terephthaloyl Chloride:** React TPA with thionyl chloride ($SOCl_2$) to form terephthaloyl chloride. This activates the carboxylic acids for amide formation and is a standard industrial process.
3.  **Amide Formation:** React terephthaloyl chloride with excess ammonia or an aqueous amine source to form terephthalamide (or its disodium salt if using NaOH). The goal is to establish the $-CONH_2$ groups in the para-position.
4.  **Hofmann Rearrangement:** Subject the terephthalamide to oxidative Hofmann rearrangement conditions (typically $NaOCl$ or $HNO_2$ under controlled basic conditions). The mechanism involves N-halogenation of the amide, migration of the phenyl ring (with the other amide group acting as an electron-withdrawing stabilizer) to the electron-deficient nitrogen, and loss of carbonate to yield p-phenylenediamine (PPD).

**Key Exploitation of Mechanism:**
The critical insight is that while TPA is deactivated toward nucleophilic attack, the amide intermediate is susceptible to the Hofmann rearrangement. The para-carboxylate/amide group stabilizes the transition state for the ring migration via inductive effects, facilitating the 1,2-shift. To ensure cost-effectiveness and selectivity:
*   **Avoid Over-halogenation:** Use stoichiometric control of the halogenating agent (e.g., NaOCl) to prevent dinitration or ring degradation.
*   **pH Control:** The reaction must be carefully buffered. Too low a pH leads to protonation of the migrating nitrogen, halting the migration step; too high a pH may cause hydrolysis of the amide back to acid. An optimal pH range of ~9–11 is hypothesized to maximize yield.
*   **Byproduct Recovery:** The carbonate byproduct ($Na_2CO_3$) can be easily separated and sold, offsetting costs.

**Why this is cost-effective:**
*   Uses waste PET as feedstock (low cost).
*   Avoids expensive catalysts (e.g., no Rh or Pt needed).
*   Hofmann rearrangement is scalable and uses cheap reagents (hypochlorite).
*   The para-selectivity is inherent to the starting material (TPA), no regioselectivity issues.

**Limitations & Mitigation:**
*   **Side Reactions:** Oxidative degradation of the ring. Mitigation: Use buffered NaOCl at low temperature (0–25°C) and short reaction times.
*   **Purity:** PPD is toxic and unstable. Mitigation: Immediate isolation under inert atmosphere, or conversion to its salt (e.g., PPD hydrochloride) for storage.

This sequence transforms a commodity polymer waste into a high-value diamine through standard, scalable organic transformations, bypassing the need for petrochemical terephthalic acid synthesis.
