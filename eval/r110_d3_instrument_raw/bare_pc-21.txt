The hypothesis centers on the formation of strong covalent bonds between the zinc oxide nanoparticles and the cellulose fiber surface through a chemical bridge, specifically utilizing citric acid as a chelating agent and cross-linker. The mechanism relies on the fact that bare cotton fibers have relatively low affinity for inorganic metal ions during precipitation, leading to weak van der Waals interactions and poor durability. By pretreating the fabric with citric acid, carboxyl groups from the acid are grafted onto the hydroxyl sites of the cellulose via esterification or hydrogen bonding. These pendant carboxylate groups act as chelating ligands, coordinating strongly with zinc ions in the precursor solution. This creates a high local concentration of zinc at the fiber interface, promoting the nucleation and growth of ZnO nanoparticles directly anchored to the textile surface. During the subsequent in situ synthesis, as the zinc hydroxide precursor decomposes to form ZnO, the citrate bridge remains, effectively locking the nanoparticle to the fiber. To exploit this, the process should involve a controlled impregnation of the cotton twill in a mixed solution of zinc acetate (or sulfate) and citric acid, followed by a thermal treatment that drives the dehydration and crystallization of ZnO without degrading the cellulose. This approach ensures uniform distribution because the nucleation sites are defined by the chemical modification of the fiber surface rather than random physical deposition, resulting in a durable, wash-resistant antimicrobial textile where the nanoparticles are chemically integrated rather than merely coated.
