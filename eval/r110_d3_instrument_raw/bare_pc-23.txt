The core mechanism to exploit is the decoupling of the nucleation rate from the crystal growth rate through the use of volatile, low-molecular-weight "modulators" or kinetic traps that reversibly block active sites on the growing crystal faces without participating in the permanent covalent bond network. By introducing a secondary component that competitively adsorbs to the reactive imine or boronate ester intermediates, one can effectively pause the polymerization chain extension on specific facets, allowing for the relaxation of strain and the ordering of monomers into the thermodynamically preferred crystalline lattice rather than kinetically trapped amorphous structures. To exploit this, one should introduce a small fraction (typically 0.5–2 mol%) of a non-polymerizable modulator—such as a monofunctional aldehyde or a bulky boronic acid derivative—into the solvothermal mixture. This modulator acts as a steric and electronic brake, slowing down the irreversible condensation reactions enough to permit defect annealing and the formation of large, well-ordered domains. Simultaneously, this kinetic suppression prevents the rapid collapse of pores during rapid shrinkage, thereby enhancing pore stability. The key is to select a modulator with a binding affinity slightly lower than the main building blocks but with a fast exchange rate, ensuring it only transiently occupies sites during the critical early stages of crystal growth, eventually being displaced by the primary monomers as the crystal matures, thus uniformly improving crystallinity, surface area, and mechanical robustness without altering the fundamental chemical composition of the final framework.
