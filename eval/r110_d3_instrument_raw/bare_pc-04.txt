The primary bottleneck for alkaline HOR on traditional noble metals is the high energy barrier associated with the breaking of the O–H bond in water (H2O ⇌ H2O* + H+ + e−), which is the rate-determining step under alkaline conditions, rather than the direct removal of adsorbed hydroxyl species as in acidic media. This sluggishness, combined with the strong adsorption of CO on Pt sites, leads to rapid catalyst poisoning and low turnover frequencies. To accelerate the kinetics while improving CO tolerance and reducing noble metal loading, I hypothesize that the mechanism must be exploited by engineering a catalyst that facilitates the "lattice oxygen" mechanism, where the O–H bond scission of water is assisted by adjacent lattice oxygen atoms on the support or within an oxide lattice, rather than relying solely on the metal's direct interaction with the adsorbate.

Specifically, I propose the design of a core-shell or alloyed nanostructure where a thin, defective oxide layer (such as IrOx or RuOx) encapsulates a highly dispersed Pt or Pd core, creating a unique interfacial environment. The key mechanism to exploit is the formation of a "hydroxyl bridge" at the metal-oxide interface. In this configuration, water molecules dissociate at the metal-oxide interfacial sites with a significantly lower activation energy because the lattice oxygen acts as a nucleophile to attack the hydrogen of the water molecule, effectively bypassing the high barrier of direct O–H cleavage on the bare metal surface. Simultaneously, the oxide component modifies the electronic structure of the metal sites via ligand effects, weakening the Pt–CO bond and enhancing the metal's tolerance to CO poisoning. By ensuring that the active sites are confined to the interfacial perimeter and that the oxide shell is amorphous or defect-rich to maximize the availability of lattice oxygen for the reaction, the catalyst can operate with a drastically reduced noble metal loading (potentially < 0.1 mg/cm²) while maintaining high activity in alkaline media. This approach decouples the activity from the bulk metal properties, relying instead on interfacial chemistry that is inherently more resistant to CO and kinetically faster in alkaline conditions due to the assisted O–H bond scission mechanism.
