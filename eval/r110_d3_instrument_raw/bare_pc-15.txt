The mechanism relies on the spatial selectivity of a cutinase or lipase enzyme, which possesses a chiral active site cavity that accommodates one enantiomer of the N-heterocyclic spiro compound significantly better than the other. In this scenario, the spiro center must be functionalized with a hydrolyzable moiety, such as a carboxylic acid ester, attached to the N-heterocyclic ring system. The enzyme selectively catalyzes the hydrolysis of the "fast-reacting" enantiomer while leaving the "slow-reacting" enantiomer intact. To exploit this for resolution and subsequent modification, one must first synthesize the racemic spiro intermediate via ring-closing metathesis or a related cyclization of a suitably functionalized diene or enyne precursor, ensuring an ester handle is present. The mixture is then subjected to the biocatalyst in the presence of water. The result is the separation of the enantiomers: the hydrolyzed enantiomer, now a carboxylic acid, can be isolated or further derivatized (e.g., via amide coupling), while the unreacted ester enantiomer remains in solution. This effectively resolves the chiral skeleton, allowing for the isolation of both enantiomers in high enantiomeric excess and enabling the divergent synthesis of modified ring systems from each enantiomeric lineage.
