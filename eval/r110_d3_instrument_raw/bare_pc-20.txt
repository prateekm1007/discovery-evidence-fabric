The active phase of NiFe-LDH catalysts during oxygen evolution in alkaline media is not the pristine layered double hydroxide but rather a dynamically formed, non-stoichiometric nickel-iron oxyhydroxide (NiFeOOH) surface layer that emerges during the initial electrochemical activation and steady-state operation. The mechanism relies on the synergistic redox coupling between Ni and Fe species, where iron plays a critical role by facilitating charge transfer and lowering the activation energy for the rate-determining water-generating step. Specifically, the incorporation of Fe into the NiOOH lattice creates a mixed-metal spinel-like electronic structure that optimizes the adsorption energies of oxygenated intermediates (OH*, O*, and OOH*) on the surface. The physical structure evolves from the ordered, brucite-like layers of the precursor into a more disordered, amorphous, or poorly crystalline oxyhydroxide with enhanced electronic conductivity. This transformation increases the density of surface-active sites and improves the availability of water molecules for reaction. To exploit this mechanism, one should focus on synthesizing precursors with specific defect engineering that promotes the rapid and extensive formation of the high-activity oxyhydroxide phase upon activation. Key factors to optimize include the Ni:Fe ratio, typically around 1:1, which maximizes the synergistic effect, and the introduction of interlayer defects or dopants that enhance ion transport and electronic coupling. Furthermore, exploiting the "electrolyte history" effect is crucial; operating the catalyst in a slightly acidic or neutral environment prior to full alkaline activation can stabilize the desired surface configuration and prevent over-oxidation or structural collapse, thereby maintaining the high density of active Fe-Ni sites responsible for superior OER performance.
