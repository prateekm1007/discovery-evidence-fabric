The hypothesis centers on the use of Deep Eutectic Solvents (DES) that serve a dual function: acting as a sustainable reaction medium and as a built-in catalyst or catalyst support. The mechanism involves the formation of strong hydrogen-bond networks between the hydrogen bond donor (typically a carboxylic acid or amine) and the hydrogen bond acceptor (such as an amine or chloride salt) within the DES. These interactions create a highly polar, tunable environment that stabilizes transition states and lowers activation energies for 1,3-azole synthesis, particularly for Hantzsch and Paal-Knorr type reactions.

To exploit this mechanism, one should design DESs where the hydrogen bond donor itself acts as a Brønsted acid or Lewis base catalyst, thereby eliminating the need for added external catalytic agents. For example, choline chloride combined with specific organic acids can create a medium that protonates carbonyls or activates nucleophiles in situ. This dual functionality allows for the replacement of toxic volatile organic solvents with a recyclable, non-volatile system. The eutectic mixture can be recovered by simple evaporation of low-boiling reactants or extraction, and reused multiple times with minimal loss of catalytic activity, thus enhancing efficiency while adhering to green chemistry principles.
