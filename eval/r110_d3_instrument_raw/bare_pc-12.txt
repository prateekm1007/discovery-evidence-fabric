The central hypothesis is that replacing bulk organic solvents with water or deep eutectic solvents, combined with mechanochemical (solid-state) processing, creates a high-energy density interface that facilitates nucleation and crystal growth without the need for prolonged thermal activation. By exploiting the high polarity and hydrogen-bonding network of water or the viscous, low-vapor-pressure nature of deep eutectics, one can stabilize reactive intermediates and facilitate ion transport for semiconductor lattice formation, but the key to maintaining material quality lies in precisely controlling the stoichiometry and mechanical milling time to prevent agglomeration and defect formation.

To exploit this mechanism for optimization, we must shift from solution-based reflux heating to a biphasic strategy: first, using aqueous or bio-based deep eutectic media at low temperatures to dissolve precursors, and second, applying intense mechanical milling under controlled humidity or in a solvent-minimized environment to drive solid-state reactions. This approach minimizes organic solvent usage by using water as a co-solvent or by operating nearly solvent-free during the milling phase. Energy consumption is reduced because mechanochemistry provides kinetic energy through mechanical impact rather than requiring the material to reach high-temperature phase transition points for diffusion. The quality of the porous semiconductor is maintained by using additives—such as biodegradable surfactants or short-chain alcohols—in minute quantities to template the pores and prevent particle sintering during the mechanical impact, ensuring the resulting material retains the high surface area and crystalline integrity required for electronics. This hybrid "green mechanochemistry" leverages the rapid diffusion kinetics of solid-state reactions while the solvent matrix controls morphology, thereby decoupling the need for high energy and toxic solvents from the achievement of high-purity semiconductor properties.
