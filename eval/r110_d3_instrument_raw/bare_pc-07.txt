To achieve simultaneous enhancement of specific binding to human lung carcinoma epithelial cells and improved biocompatibility in dermal fibroblast cell lines, the most effective chemical modification strategy involves conjugating folic acid to a squaraine dye via a hydrophilic, biocompatible spacer arm, while simultaneously introducing steric shielding or zwitterionic groups on the periphery of the squaraine core.

The mechanism relies on exploiting differential receptor expression. Human lung carcinoma cells frequently overexpress the reduced folate carrier (RFC) or folate receptor alpha (FRA) on their cell surfaces, whereas normal dermal fibroblasts typically express these receptors at significantly lower levels. By attaching folic acid, the photosensitizer gains active, receptor-mediated endocytosis in the target cancer cells, drastically increasing intracellular accumulation and photodynamic efficacy in the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, in fibroblast lines, the lack of high-density folate receptors means the conjugate does not undergo active uptake; instead, it remains largely extracellular or enters via non-specific diffusion at low rates.

To specifically improve biocompatibility in the fibroblasts, the spacer arm between the squaraine and folic acid should be engineered to be highly hydrophilic and flexible, such as a polyethylene glycol (PEG) chain or a polyamine derivative. This hydrophilicity minimizes non-specific hydrophobic interactions with the lipid bilayers of fibroblast membranes, reducing membrane disruption and cytotoxicity. Furthermore, the squaraine core itself can be modified with bulky, neutral steric groups (e.g., tert-butyl or fluorenyl units) or zwitterionic moieties to prevent aggregation-caused quenching and to create a hydration shell that repels proteins and cells in normal tissues.

Thus, the hypothesis is that this dual modification creates a "smart" photosensitizer where high-affinity active targeting drives accumulation in folate-overexpressing carcinoma cells, while the hydrophilic shielding and steric bulk ensure that in folate-sparse fibroblasts, the molecule is sterically hindered from causing significant membrane damage or being efficiently taken up, thereby preserving dermal tissue integrity during photodynamic therapy. This strategy exploits the biological heterogeneity of folate receptor expression combined with physical-chemical principles of steric hindrance and hydrophilicity to achieve therapeutic selectivity.
