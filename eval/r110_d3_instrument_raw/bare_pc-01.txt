The core hypothesis is that the formation of nanoparticles or porous structures via solvent exchange is governed by the position of the polymer solution relative to the binodal curve in the ternary phase diagram, specifically how the diffusion rate of the nonsolvent interacts with the kinetics of phase separation. To exploit this mechanism, one must map the solubility of the copolymer as a function of solvent concentration, temperature, and composition to identify the spinodal and binodal regions. The process involves initially dissolving the copolymer in a good solvent, creating a homogeneous single phase. When the nonsolvent is introduced via solvent exchange, the local composition of the mixture shifts. If the shift crosses the binodal curve, the system becomes thermodynamically unstable, triggering a liquid-liquid phase separation. The morphology of the resulting structures—whether it forms discrete nanoparticles or a continuous porous network—depends on whether the phase separation occurs via nucleation-and-growth or spinodal decomposition. Nucleation-and-growth typically yields nanoparticles when the system enters the metastable region slowly, allowing distinct droplets to form and coalesce. Conversely, rapid entry into the unstable region leads to spinodal decomposition, which creates a bicontinuous network that can be frozen into a porous structure.

For copolymers containing basic amino groups, the processing behavior is further modulated by the ionizable nature of the chain. The solubility of these polymers is pH-dependent; in acidic environments or with acidic degradation products, the amino groups become protonated, increasing the hydrophilicity of the polymer and potentially enhancing its interaction with the nonsolvent (water). This specific chemical behavior allows for precise tuning of the phase diagram boundaries. By adjusting the pH or the ratio of acidic to non-acidic comonomers, one can shift the critical points on the phase diagram, thereby controlling the critical composition required for phase separation. Exploiting this means designing the copolymer composition to have a steep solubility gradient near the target processing conditions, which ensures a sharp, predictable transition from a homogeneous solution to a phase-separated state, enabling the targeted formation of either sub-micron nanoparticles for drug delivery or macroporous scaffolds for tissue engineering. The key to successful mapping is characterizing the equilibrium phase diagram through methods like cloud point determination or DSC, and then correlating these thermodynamic limits with kinetic factors like diffusion rates to predict the final morphology.
