To mitigate the degradation of ethylene-vinyl alcohol (EVOH) barrier properties caused by humidity plasticization while maintaining a Water Vapor Transmission Rate (WVTR) below 0.5 g/m²/day, the most robust engineering intervention is to apply an **impermeable, chemically inert interlayer barrier** between the EVOH layer and the surrounding environment.

### Mitigation Proposal

**Mechanism:**
The core issue is that EVOH is hygroscopic; when exposed to high humidity (65% RH), water molecules adsorb onto the polymer chains, increasing chain mobility and free volume (plasticization). This disrupts the tortuous path and free-volume constraints that provide EVOH’s excellent barrier properties, causing WVTR to rise. The proposed solution is to encapsulate the EVOH layer within a multilayer film structure by inserting a **Polyamide (PA) or Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) interlayer** with a thickness sufficient to provide negligible moisture permeation relative to the EVOH layer, and then sealing the entire structure with an outer **Polyethylene (PE) skin** layer. 

More specifically, the design change is to **replace the direct exposure of the EVOH layer to ambient humidity during distribution with a hermetic, low-permeability outer shell**. While PA and PET have good moisture barriers, they are superior to EVOH in their resistance to plasticization by water. By placing a 30-micron PA layer on the outside of the EVOH layer (and potentially another 50-micron PA or PET layer on the inside, depending on the lamination structure), the PA/PET layers act as "moisture shields." Because PA and PET have a much lower equilibrium moisture uptake and their free volume is less sensitive to adsorbed water molecules compared to EVOH, the plasticization effect on the EVOH layer is effectively blocked. The moisture concentration at the interface of the EVOH-PA layer remains low and stable, preserving the EVOH's structural integrity and barrier performance.

**Implementation:**
1.  **Redesign the Laminate Structure:** Modify the existing multilayer film (likely PE/EVOH/PE) to a **PE/EVOH/PA/PE** or **PET/EVOH/PA/PE** structure. The key is that the PA layer must be in direct contact with the EVOH layer on the side exposed to the external environment.
2.  **Increase PA Thickness:** Use a minimum of 30-micron (1.2 mil) PA as the outer moisture barrier layer. PA has a WVTR of approximately 0.1–0.3 g/m²/day at 65% RH, which is lower than the target limit of 0.5 g/m²/day even *before* considering the contribution of the EVOH layer. When combined in series, the total system WVTR is governed by the sum of resistances. Since the PA layer provides high resistance and is stable, the EVOH layer is protected from plasticization.
3.  **Seal the Edges:** Ensure the multilayer film is hermetically sealed (e.g., via induction heat sealing) to prevent edge seepage, which is often a dominant failure mode in barrier films.

**Measures it Relies On:**
*   **Pa’s Stability:** Polyamide (specifically PA-6) has a very low water sorption equilibrium and its transport properties are far less sensitive to RH changes than EVOH. It does not undergo significant plasticization in the same manner.
*   **Series Resistance Principle:** In a multilayer film, the total permeance is the inverse of the sum of the resistances. By adding a stable, high-barrier layer (PA) in series with the unstable layer (EVOH), the total system's performance becomes dominated by the stable layer's resistance, effectively "shielding" the EVOH from humidity fluctuations.
*   **Negligible Moisture Ingress to EVOH:** The low permeability of PA ensures that the relative humidity experienced at the EVOH-PA interface remains significantly lower than the ambient 65% RH, keeping the EVOH in a "dry" state where its barrier properties are optimal.

### Concrete Test to Falsify the Proposed Approach

To falsify this approach, one must demonstrate that the proposed PA-encapsulated structure still fails to meet the constraint under the specified conditions.

**Test Protocol:**
1.  **Sample Preparation:** Fabricate a 1-meter roll of the proposed **PE/EVOH/PA(30μm)/PE** multilayer film.
2.  **Conditioning:** Equilibrate the samples at 25°C and 65% relative humidity for 48 hours.
3.  **Measurement:** Use a **Custodion® O2/WVTR** analyzer (or equivalent gravimetric/desiccator method) to measure the Water Vapor Transmission Rate (WVTR) at 25°C and 65% RH.
4.  **Falsification Criteria:**
    *   If the measured **WVTR exceeds 0.5 g/m²/day**, the proposed mitigation is falsified. This would indicate that the PA layer is either too thin, has insufficient barrier properties at that RH, or that the EVOH layer is still being plasticized due to moisture migrating through the PA or that the lamination process introduced defects (voids, delamination) that bypass the PA shield.
    *   Alternatively, if the **oxygen transmission rate (OTR)** rises significantly (indicating that moisture ingress has plasticized the EVOH layer, reducing its oxygen barrier as well, even if the WVTR test passes by a narrow margin), this would also falsify the design's long-term reliability for shelf-life, as oxygen degradation often precedes visible moisture issues in food packaging. However, the primary constraint is WVTR, so the direct falsification is a WVTR measurement > 0.5 g/m²/day.

In practice, this test would likely pass (WVTR < 0.5) because PA-6 alone typically has a WVTR of ~0.2-0.3 g/m²/day at 65% RH, and the EVOH layer adds further resistance. If it fails, it would be due to manufacturing defects (e.g., pinholes in the PA layer) or an unexpected synergy effect where moisture *does* diffuse through PA faster than expected at high RH, invalidating the assumption that PA shields the EVOH.
