**Mitigation Proposal: Electrochemical Potential Stabilization via Surface Barrier Engineering**

**1. Mechanism**
The failure mode, Potential-Induced Degradation (PID) caused by sodium ion migration, is driven by two factors: (1) the presence of mobile alkali metal ions (Na⁺) in the encapsulant or at the glass interface, and (2) the system voltage bias (1000 V) which creates an electric field driving these ions into the cell junction, causing shunting.

The proposed mitigation is **not** to remove the existing encapsulant (violating the constraint) but to **prevent the ingress and migration of mobile ions** by modifying the **electrical boundary conditions at the module edges** and **blocking the ion source path** at the glass-cell interface. This is achieved through a **non-invasive, post-manufacturing application of a high-resistivity, ion-blocking dielectric barrier coating** on the module’s perimeter and back-side glass, combined with **elevated back-side potential** relative to the frame.

*   **Primary Action:** Apply a transparent, high-resistivity (>10¹⁵ Ω·cm), ion-impermeable dielectric coating (e.g., parylene-C or a specialized fluoropolymer) to the *exposed* edges of the cover glass and the backsheet perimeter. This prevents ambient moisture and sodium-containing particles from entering the edge seal, which is a primary vector for ion contamination over time.
*   **Secondary Action (Crucial for PID):** Implement **Back-Side Grounding/Elevation**: Electrically isolate the module’s active area from the aluminum frame. Instead of grounding the backsheet or frame (which sets the module potential to 0 V relative to earth, creating a strong field driving Na⁺ *into* the cell at 1000 V string voltage), the backsheet is **insulated** from the frame and **biased to the same potential as the string** (or slightly higher) via a dedicated, high-voltage-rated feed-through or conductive path that does not cross the front glass. This reduces the electric field gradient across the glass/encapsulant interface from ~1000 V to near 0 V, effectively eliminating the driving force for Na⁺ migration. *Note: This requires a specific design modification to the backsheet/frame interface, but it does not involve replacing the encapsulant.*

**2. Implementation**
*   **Step 1 (Backside Insulation & Biasing):** Modify the module’s backsheet assembly. Use a high-voltage-rated, non-conductive backsheet material that is electrically isolated from the aluminum frame by a ≥6 mm air gap or solid insulating spacer. Connect the backsheet to a conductive busbar that is itself connected to the string’s negative terminal (or the string potential) via a dedicated, high-voltage insulated cable that does not pass through the front glass. This ensures the potential difference between the front glass and backside is minimized. *In high-voltage strings, the "bias" refers to the voltage difference between the module and its surroundings. By floating the backside to the string potential, you eliminate the 1000 V drop across the glass thickness.*
*   **Step 2 (Edge Sealing):** Apply a parylene-C coating (2-5 µm thick) to all exposed glass edges and the frame-to-glass interface using vapor deposition. This coating is highly conformal, chemically inert, and has negligible ion permeability, blocking the primary leakage path for sodium ions from the environment.
*   **Step 3 (No Encapsulant Replacement):** The existing EVA or POE encapsulant remains in place. The mitigation relies on external electrical and boundary conditioning.

**3. Measures It Relies On**
*   **Electric Field Reduction:** The core measure is reducing the electrostatic stress (V/m) across the glass/encapsulant interface by minimizing the potential difference between the front and back of the module.
*   **Ion Blocking:** The parylene-C edge coating blocks the ingress of exogenous sodium ions and moisture, which are necessary for the conduction path that sustains PID.
*   **Long-Term Stability:** Parylene-C is stable for >25 years and does not outgass ions.

**4. Concrete Falsification Test**
To falsify this approach, conduct a **accelerated PID test under extreme conditions with the mitigation applied:**

1.  **Specimen:** 10 modules treated with the backside insulation/biasing and edge parylene-C coating.
2.  **Conditions:** Submerge the *backside* of the modules in a 5% NaCl solution (simulating worst-case sodium ion source) while maintaining a **1000 V DC bias** between the front busbar and the *unbiased* frame (simulating the failure mode where the backside is grounded, i.e., the *worst-case* for the mitigation if the backside biasing fails or is not effective). **Crucially, the test must verify that the mitigation *works* by showing that when the backside is properly biased to the string potential, PID does not occur. Therefore, the falsification test is twofold:**
    *   **Test A (Positive Control - Mitigation Active):** Maintain the backside at the string potential. Subject to 1000 V bias, high humidity (85% RH), and 65°C for 72 hours. **Falsification:** If power loss exceeds 0.5% after a 25-year equivalent accelerated stress (e.g., using a thermal-electrical model to extrapolate), the approach fails.
    *   **Test B (Falsification of Mechanism - Mitigation Removed/Ineffective):** Take a similar control group where the backside is *grounded* (standard practice that causes PID) and the edge coating is *scraped off*. Subject to the same conditions. **Expected Result:** Significant PID (>5% loss).
    *   **Final Falsification Criterion:** If the **mitigated** group (Test A) shows an annualized power loss >0.5% when extrapolated over 25 years using standard reliability models (e.g., Arrhenius for temperature, Arrhenius-Curie for voltage stress), the proposal is falsified. Specifically, if the backside biasing does not sufficiently reduce the interfacial electric field, and Na⁺ migration continues despite the edge coating, the mitigation fails.
