**Mitigation Proposal: Electrostatically Charged, Superhydrophilic Zirconium Dioxide (ZrO₂) Surface Treatment**

### Mechanism
The primary driver of biofilm and scale accumulation on stainless steel (SS316L) plates is the initial adhesion of organic matter, bacteria, and nucleation of calcium carbonate. By applying a thin conformal layer of zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) with a high density of surface hydroxyl groups via Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD), we create a surface that is **superhydrophilic** (contact angle < 10°). 

When in contact with the district cooling water, this superhydrophilic layer induces a **hydration layer effect**. Instead of water forming a thin, stagnant film, it forms a highly ordered, tightly bound multi-layer of water molecules that acts as an entropic and steric barrier against protein adsorption and bacterial attachment (similar to the "lotus effect" but for water-loving surfaces). Furthermore, the specific crystal phase (monoclinic ZrO₂) can be tuned to present a slight negative surface potential, repelling the negatively charged cell walls of most bacteria and inhibiting the initial nucleation of calcium carbonate crystals, which requires a neutral/positive substrate for stable adhesion.

### Implementation
1.  **Surface Preparation:** Mechanical brushing and chemical etching of the stainless steel plate channels to increase roughness and ensure adhesion of the next layer.
2.  **ALD Coating Process:** Install the heat exchanger plates in a dedicated ALD chamber. Deposit a conformal ZrO₂ layer of approximately 10–20 nm thickness using ZrCl₄ and H₂O precursors at low temperatures (<150°C) to avoid distorting the metal.
3.  **Post-Treatment:** Anneal the coated plates to stabilize the hydration layer. The resulting surface has a water contact angle < 10°.
4.  **Integration:** Assemble the exchanger with standard gaskets. No changes to the hydraulic flow rate, temperature differentials, or water chemistry (beyond the existing non-chlorinated limit) are required.

### Measures Relied Upon
*   **Thermodynamic Barrier:** The hydration layer prevents the "dry patch" formation that allows bacteria to attach and scale to nucleate.
*   **Reduced Friction & Fouling Factor:** The smooth, hard ZrO₂ layer resists mechanical wear and provides a hard surface that is less prone to abrasive scale build-up than bare stainless steel.
*   **Sustained Performance:** The coating is chemically stable in the pH range of district cooling water (typically 6.5–8.5) and does not leach toxic substances, complying with the <0.5 ppm chlorination constraint (no oxidation reactions needed for cleaning).

### Falsification Test
**Test Name:** *In-Situ Biofouling and Scale Adhesion Resistance Benchmark*

1.  **Setup:** Create two parallel, identical test loops of stainless steel heat exchanger plates:
    *   **Control Loop:** Bare stainless steel plates with standard industrial biofilm/scale inhibitors (but no chlorination >0.5 ppm).
    *   **Test Loop:** ALD-ZrO₂ coated plates with identical inhibitors and water chemistry.
2.  **Procedure:** Circulate district cooling water (or a simulated water with known biological load and hardness) at the design flow velocity for 12 months without mechanical cleaning. Monitor the overall heat transfer coefficient ($U$) and pressure drop ($\Delta P$) continuously via instrumentation.
3.  **Falsification Criteria:**
    *   **Success:** The Test Loop maintains $U > 2500$ W/m²K for the full 12 months AND the pressure drop increase $\Delta P$ remains below the baseline design margin for the same period.
    *   **Failure (Falsification):** If the Test Loop shows $U$ dropping below 2500 W/m²K at any point before 12 months, OR if significant biofilm/scale adhesion is observed post-inspection (e.g., via endoscopy) that cannot be removed by the standard non-chlorinated chemical flush, the mitigation is falsified.
    *   **Specific Biofouling Check:** If the Control Loop achieves the target but the Test Loop fails, it proves the coating is ineffective. If both fail, it proves the inhibitor regime is insufficient, not necessarily the coating. However, for the *coating* to be considered a valid mitigation for *this* failure mode, it must outperform the bare steel under the same non-chlorinated constraint. If the coated plates do not show a statistically significant delay in the onset of $U$ degradation compared to bare steel, the mechanism is falsified.
