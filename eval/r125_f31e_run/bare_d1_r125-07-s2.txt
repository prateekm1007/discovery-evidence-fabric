**Mitigation Proposal: Thermal-Dependent Pressure Compensation via Ammeter-to-Load Mapping**

**Mechanism:**
Implement a closed-loop control strategy that dynamically adjusts the pump’s pressure setpoint (via the existing hydraulic regulator/relief valve control, if electrically adjustable, or by modulating the motor’s effective load) based on real-time thermal state and electrical load signatures. The core insight is that rising oil viscosity due to heat causes the pump to work harder (higher ammeter reading) for the same hydraulic output, leading to a "stall" effect where pressure falls off as the oil thins and internal leakage increases. By correlating the motor ammeter (current) with the expected pressure requirement, we can anticipate the pressure drop before it becomes critical. Specifically, if the ammeter reads higher than expected for a given stroke phase *and* the thermometers indicate elevated temperature, the system reduces the target pressure setpoint slightly or extends the dwell time, preventing the pump from hitting its maximum capacity limit and stalling, which causes the pressure fall-off.

**Implementation:**
1.  **Baseline Calibration:** During a cold-start, long-duration test, log the motor ammeter current against time and stroke position (using the hour-run counter and external position encoder if available, or inferring stroke phase from current waveform patterns) at three distinct temperature points measured by the reservoir and manifold thermometers (e.g., 40°C, 55°C, 70°C).
2.  **Create Compensation Curve:** Using this data, establish a mathematical relationship: *Target Pressure Adjustment Factor* = f(Ammeter Current, Reservoir Temp, Manifold Temp). For instance, if the manifold temp exceeds 60°C, the system automatically lowers the nominal pressure setpoint by 10% and increases the pump dwell time by 15% to allow for higher flow demands without stalling.
3.  **Active Monitoring & Adjustment:** During operation, the controller continuously monitors the ammeter. If the current spike exceeds the thermal-corrected threshold (higher current at high temps indicates increased friction/leakage), it proactively reduces the pressure demand or opens the relief valve slightly *before* the stroke ends. This prevents the "pressure fall-off" symptom by avoiding the point where the pump cannot maintain pressure against the system’s back-pressure and thermal expansion effects.
4.  **Hour-Run Counter Logic:** Tie this compensation logic to the hour-run counter. After every 500 hours, automatically trigger a re-calibration cycle (a 10-minute test at varying temperatures) to update the compensation curve, accounting for wear in the pump and valves that changes the current-temperature relationship over time.

**Measures Relied On:**
*   **Pump Motor Ammeter:** Used as a proxy for real-time mechanical load and friction/leakage state. A rising current at constant pressure indicates increased resistance (thermal thinning + wear).
*   **Reservoir & Manifold Thermometers:** Used to determine the thermal state of the oil. Manifold temp reflects the immediate working condition, while reservoir temp indicates the bulk temperature and heat dissipation status. Their differential helps identify if the cooler (if any) is failing or if the load is simply too high for the thermal capacity.
*   **Hour-Run Counter:** Used as a trigger for periodic re-calibration of the compensation curve, ensuring the control logic adapts to gradual wear and aging without needing new sensors.

**Outcome:**
This method leverages existing sensors to implement predictive pressure management. Instead of allowing the pressure to fall off at the end of the stroke due to thermal-induced performance degradation, the system actively compensates by adjusting pressure targets and timing based on real-time thermal and electrical load data, maintaining consistent performance throughout long duty cycles without new instrumentation.
