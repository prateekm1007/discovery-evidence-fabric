**Mitigation Proposal: Feed-Forward Compensation with Dry-Bulb Outlet Trending**

**Mechanism:**
Decouple the reactive moisture control loop from the primary control of heat input. Instead of letting the slow, late-acting moisture probe dictate steam/heat changes, use the fast-responding **dry-bulb outlet temperature trend** as a lead indicator. When the exit dry-bulb temperature starts to climb *against* the existing control bias at a constant feed rate, it signals that the specific heat requirement per unit mass is decreasing or the evaporation capacity is exceeding the current moisture load, allowing latent heat to convert to sensible heat (temperature rise). This indicates that the current heat input is "too high" for the actual moisture content being processed, or that the feed characteristics have changed (e.g., incoming moisture was lower than expected, requiring less heat, or the dryer is running too hot for the desired exit profile).

Conversely, if moisture creeps up, the exit dry-bulb temperature typically *drops* (more heat is consumed as latent heat for evaporation) or the wet-bulb depression changes. However, the problem states "moisture creeps above spec... exit temperature has been climbing." This is a critical diagnostic clue: **Climbing exit temperature at constant feed rate usually indicates that the heat input is excessive relative to the evaporation capacity, OR that the feed is drier than assumed (so less latent heat is consumed, causing sensible temp to rise), yet the final product is *moister*?** 

Wait, if the product is *more* moist, less evaporation occurs? No, if the product is *more* moist, *more* evaporation should occur, consuming *more* heat, causing exit temperature to *drop*. If exit temperature is *climbing* while product moisture is *rising*, this implies the dryer is **thermally throttled** or **starved of airflow**, but more likely, the control loop has been over-driving heat to compensate for a perceived (but actual false) lack of moisture, or the heat source is running away. 

Actually, a common scenario: The moisture controller sees high moisture and adds heat. The added heat causes the exit dry-bulb to climb rapidly. If the moisture sensor is lagging, the controller may have already over-corrected. The climbing exit temp is a sign of **excessive heat input** that is not being used for evaporation (perhaps because the feed is already dry enough in the middle stages, or airflow is restricted). The result is product moisture rising? No, excess heat usually lowers moisture. 

Let's re-read carefully: "Product moisture creeps above spec... exit temperature has been climbing." This is counter-intuitive for a simple heat/moisture balance. It suggests that the **heat addition is not effectively contacting the product** (e.g., poor airflow distribution, channeling, or sticking) OR that the **feed rate has actually increased** (but problem says "same feed rate"). 

Another possibility: The "climbing exit temperature" is a symptom of the control system's **integral windup** or a **gain too high** that is destabilizing the process. The moisture loop reacts late, so it adds heat in large, slow increments. The exit temperature rises as a result of the accumulated heat. If the product is getting *wetter*, it might be because the residence time has decreased (drum speed up? not stated) or the evaporation is happening in the inlet zone only, and the exit zone is over-heating the product surface, causing "hardening" that traps moisture. 

Given the constraint "use only existing sensors (wet/dry bulb in/out, feed rate)", the most robust mitigation is to **implement a feed-forward correction based on the rate of change of the exit dry-bulb temperature** to modulate the heat input *before* the moisture loop reacts, and to **limit the maximum exit dry-bulb temperature** to prevent overheating that can cause moisture trapping or sensor errors.

**Implementation:**
1.  **Define a Dry-Bulb Temperature Envelope:** Using historical data, map the expected exit dry-bulb temperature versus the desired product moisture for the current feed rate. Establish a **maximum allowable exit dry-bulb setpoint** ($T_{dry,max}$) that correlates with the specification limit of product moisture. If the exit dry-bulb temperature exceeds this envelope, it indicates excessive sensible heat addition that is not contributing to evaporation (or is causing adverse effects).
2.  **Decouple Heat Control:** Modify the heat input controller (steam valve or burner) to use a **split-range or cascaded logic**:
    *   **Primary (Fast) Control:** Use the **exit dry-bulb temperature** as the primary fast-acting variable for heat input adjustment. If $T_{dry,exit}$ is trending up towards $T_{dry,max}$, *immediately* trim down the heat input, regardless of the moisture controller's demand. This prevents the "climbing temperature" from causing overheating or control instability.
    *   **Secondary (Slow) Correction:** Use the **exit wet-bulb temperature** (which is a better proxy for moisture content in the gas phase) as a slow feed-forward bias for the heat input. The wet-bulb depression ($T_{dry} - T_{wet}$) at the outlet is directly related to the humidity of the exhaust gas, which reflects the evaporation rate. If the wet-bulb depression is too low, it means the gas is saturated (evaporation is efficient). If it is too high, evaporation may be limited. Use this to fine-tune the baseline heat setpoint.
3.  **Feed-Forward from Feed Rate:** Scale the heat input setpoint directly proportional to the feed rate meter reading. If feed rate is constant, the baseline heat should be constant. Any deviation in the calculated required heat should be investigated.

**Measures it Relies On:**
1.  **Exit Dry-Bulb Temperature Trending:** Detects the "climbing" anomaly in real-time and allows for proactive reduction of heat input before the slow moisture loop makes a large, late correction. It acts as a "thermal ceiling" to prevent overheating.
2.  **Exit Wet-Bulb Temperature (Wet-Bulb Depression):** Provides a faster, more direct measure of the evaporation capacity of the dryer than the final product moisture probe. The wet-bulb temperature at the outlet is a strong indicator of how much moisture has been removed from the gas stream. A stable wet-bulb depression at a given feed rate indicates stable evaporation.
3.  **Feed Rate Metering:** Ensures that the heat input is not inadvertently mismatched to the mass flow of material. It serves as the normalizing factor for the heat setpoint.

By prioritizing the fast, thermodynamic indicators (dry-bulb temperature trend and wet-bulb depression) over the slow, direct product moisture measurement, the control system can prevent the exit temperature from climbing and stabilize the process, which will in turn bring the product moisture back into spec by ensuring heat is applied efficiently without overheating the final stages of the dryer.
