Mitigation Proposal: Closed-Loop Suction/Discharge Pressure Differential Monitoring with Automated VFD Setpoint Adjustment

**Mechanism:**
The decay in developed head at a constant flow rate indicates pump performance degradation (likely due to impeller wear, cavitation, or increased hydraulic resistance). Simultaneously, the motor draws higher amps than baseline to maintain the required discharge pressure, indicating increased hydraulic load or inefficiency. The core issue is that the pump is working harder for the same output. The mitigation leverages the *existing* sensors to detect the performance drift and automatically adjust the pump's operating point to compensate for the loss in efficiency, thereby reducing the electrical load on the motor back toward baseline levels while maintaining the required system pressure.

**Implementation:**
1.  **Calculate Real-Time Efficiency Proxy:** Using the existing Venturi flow meter (providing Flow Rate $Q$), the suction pressure gauge ($P_s$), and the discharge pressure gauge ($P_d$), calculate the differential pressure $\Delta P = P_d - P_s$. Establish a baseline curve of $\Delta P$ vs. $Q$ from commissioning data.
2.  **Detect Drift:** Monitor the deviation between the current measured $\Delta P$ at a specific $Q$ and the baseline $\Delta P$. If the current $\Delta P$ is lower than baseline at the same $Q$ (indicating head decay), a drift is detected. Simultaneously, monitor the motor ammeter. If amps are higher than baseline for the same $P_d$ and $Q$, confirm the inefficiency.
3.  **Automated VFD Adjustment:** If a VFD is already installed on the pump motor (a common assumption for "key" pumps with ammeter monitoring), implement a simple control logic:
    *   Set the control objective to maintain the *required* discharge pressure ($P_d$) and *required* flow ($Q$).
    *   When the controller detects that the current VFD frequency (speed) is causing the motor amps to exceed baseline by a set margin (e.g., >5%) while maintaining the target $P_d$ and $Q$, it indicates the pump curve has shifted.
    *   *Correction Logic:* Since head decay means the pump is less efficient, we cannot simply lower speed. Instead, the system must accept that more work is required. The mitigation is not to "fix" the mechanical issue (which we can't do without shutdown), but to *optimize the operating point* to minimize energy waste.
    *   **Crucial Adjustment:** The prompt asks to mitigate the *failure*. The failure is "decayed head" causing "higher amps." The direct mitigation for the *electrical* symptom (high amps) while maintaining performance is to ensure the pump is not operating in a throttled or inefficient region due to control lag. However, if the head is physically decayed, the only way to get the same head at the same flow is to *increase* speed, which *increases* amps further. Therefore, the mitigation must focus on *preventing further degradation* or *compensating for system-side issues*.
    *   *Revised Mechanism based on "Existing Sensors Only":* The high amps at the same discharge pressure and flow suggest the pump is slipping or the system resistance has increased (e.g., clogged strainer, closed valve partially). The Venturi meter measures actual flow. If the control loop is trying to hold a setpoint, and amps are high, it might be over-driving.
    *   **Better Mitigation: Cavitation Detection and Suction Pressure Boosting.** High amps and head decay are classic signs of cavitation or low NPSH available. Cavitation reduces head and increases vibration/current fluctuations (though here it's steady high amps). The existing suction pressure gauge can detect low suction pressure.
    *   **Final Mechanism:** **Implement a Suction Pressure Threshold Alarm and Automatic Flow Reduction.**
        *   If the suction pressure ($P_s$) drops below a calculated NPSH margin threshold (derived from $P_s$, temperature if inferable, or just a fixed safety margin from baseline), AND motor amps are >110% of baseline, trigger a "Protective Slowdown."
        *   The control system automatically reduces the VFD speed (if available) or, if no VFD, signals a process operator to open a bypass or reduce load, while simultaneously monitoring the discharge pressure. If discharge pressure is held by a pressure-control valve, the valve may be throttling.
        *   *Actually, the most robust mitigation with *only* these sensors to address "high amps for same P/Q" is to identify if the "same flow" is actually the *measured* flow from the Venturi. If the Venturi is dirty, it reads low, causing the controller to overdrive the pump to achieve a "perceived" flow or pressure. But the constraint says "same flow" has decayed head.
        *   Let's stick to the most likely engineering cause: **Impeller Erosion/Wear.** This cannot be fixed without shutdown. The mitigation for the *operational symptom* (high energy consumption/amps) is to **re-balance the operating point to avoid the peak power consumption region** if the system curve has also shifted.
        *   **Proposed Mitigation:** **Implement a Power-Limiting Control Loop based on Ampere Thresholds.**
            1.  Use the motor ammeter to establish a strict upper limit on current (e.g., 95% of FLA).
            2.  Integrate this limit into the pump's control logic (VFD or valve control).
            3.  If the pump reaches the required discharge pressure but the motor current exceeds the baseline threshold (indicating efficiency loss), the control system prioritizes *current limitation* over *pressure precision*. It will allow the discharge pressure to drop slightly to bring the current back to normal levels, preventing motor overheating and tripping. This is a "soft" mitigation that prevents catastrophic motor failure while flagging the need for pump overhaul.
            4.  *Alternatively*, if the "decay" is due to system-side issues like a partially closed discharge valve detected by the pressure/flow relationship, use the **Venturi meter and Pressure Gauges to perform an automated "Pump Curve Diagnostics" check every 24 hours.** If the $\Delta P$ vs. $Q$ relationship deviates from baseline, log a critical maintenance ticket. This doesn't fix it immediately but mitigates the risk of undetected failure leading to motor burnout.

**Mitigation: Automatic Motor Current Limiter with Suction Pressure Verification**

**Mechanism:**
The high motor current indicates the pump is operating inefficiently due to head decay. To mitigate the risk of motor overheating and failure while maintaining process cooling, implement a current-limiting control strategy that uses the existing ammeter to cap the motor load, and uses the suction pressure gauge to verify the pump is not cavitating (which would also cause high current and head loss). If the pump is cavitating, the suction pressure will be abnormally low.

**Implementation:**
1.  **Current Limiting:** Configure the VFD (if present) or add a simple overload relay reset interlock (if no VFD, but this is risky) to limit the maximum motor current to 90% of the Full Load Amperes (FLA). This prevents the motor from drawing excessive amps that could lead to thermal damage or trip, even if it means the discharge pressure will temporarily drop.
2.  **Cavitation Check Logic:** Create a simple boolean logic in the DCS/PLC using the existing sensors:
    *   IF (Motor Current > 105% of Baseline) AND (Suction Pressure < [Baseline Suction Pressure - 5%]), THEN trigger "Suspected Cavitation or Suction Restriction" alarm.
    *   Action: This alarm prompts operators to check suction strainers/valves, which is the most common fix for sudden head decay and high amps.
