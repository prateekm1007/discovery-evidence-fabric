The central hypothesis is that the driving force for cooperative palladium catalysis in these nonaromatic systems is not the restoration of aromaticity, as seen in traditional arene-based Catellani chemistry, but rather the formation of a thermodynamically stable, trisubstituted alkene conjugated with a heteroatom or embedded within a rigid cyclic framework. In traditional arene cycles, the oxidative cyclization is irreversible because it generates a stable aromatic system. For heterocycles like uracil, quinolones, or pyridones, and strained cyclic ethers like glycals, the analogous step is the formation of a highly substituted, stereodefined alkene that is locked by ring strain or steric hindrance.

To exploit this, one must utilize norbornene (NBE) not just as a generic carbodiene precursor, but as a specific allyl-Pd partner that facilitates a [3,3]-sigmatropic rearrangement or a transmetalation step leading to a stable alkene intermediate. The mechanism likely proceeds through initial oxidative addition of a halogenated heterocycle or ether into Pd(0) to form a Pd(II)-aryl or Pd(II)-alkyl species. This species then undergoes migratory insertion into the norbornene double bond, generating a tertiary alkyl-Pd intermediate. The key step is the reductive elimination or beta-hydride elimination that yields the alkene product. Unlike arenes, where the product is planar and aromatic, the alkene products in heterocycles and cyclic ethers are chiral and rigid. 

Exploiting this requires careful control of regioselectivity during the insertion step. For heterocycles, the heteroatom (O, N, S) must coordinate to the palladium center to stabilize the transition state and direct the insertion toward the less substituted carbon of the norbornene, ensuring the bulky heterocyclic group ends up in the less hindered position. For glycals, the ring strain and the adjacent oxygen substituent create a specific electronic environment that favors a particular stereoelectronic alignment. By using bulky ligands on palladium, such as SPhos or XPhos, one can enforce the necessary steric pressure to prevent isomerization of the alkene product and drive the reaction toward the desired stable alkene isomer. The stability of the final alkene, derived from its trisubstituted nature and the rigidity of the ring, provides the thermodynamic sink that makes the reaction irreversible, mimicking the role of aromaticity in classical Catellani chemistry.
