The primary kinetic bottleneck in alkaline media is not merely the thermodynamics of hydrogen adsorption, but the high activation energy associated with water dissociation to generate adsorbed hydroxide intermediates. Therefore, the central hypothesis is that rapid hydrogen production in alkaline electrolytes requires a bifunctional mechanism where the electrocatalyst surface simultaneously facilitates rapid water dissociation through specific metal-hydroxyl coordination sites, while adjacent sites provide optimal hydrogen binding energies for the subsequent Volmer-Heyrovsky or Volmer-Tafel steps. 

To exploit this, electrocatalysts—specifically pyrolyzed MOFs or their derivatives—should be engineered to create a synergistic interface between metal oxides and nitrogen-doped carbon. The metal oxide components serve as the active sites for water dissociation, effectively lowering the barrier for forming adsorbed H and OH species by stabilizing the transition state through specific coordination geometries. Simultaneously, the surrounding carbon matrix or neighboring metal atoms must be tuned to modulate the electronic density of the active sites, ensuring that the adsorbed hydrogen atoms do not bind too strongly or too weakly. This creates a spatially resolved environment where water activation occurs rapidly at metal-rich sites, and hydrogen recombination or desorption proceeds efficiently at sites with optimized d-band centers, thereby decoupling the slow water dissociation step from the hydrogen evolution step and overcoming the intrinsic kinetic limitation of alkaline HER.
