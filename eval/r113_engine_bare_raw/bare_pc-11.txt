The hypothesis centers on exploiting the intrinsic mechanical and electronic properties of single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) to enable rapid, mild, and scalable defect formation through **high-power, low-frequency ultrasonic cavitation combined with transient radical generation from benign oxidants**.

Specifically, we propose that intense ultrasound applied to aqueous or alcohol-based SWCNT dispersions generates microbubbles that implode violently. This implosion creates localized "hotspots" with extreme temperatures and pressures, but for a fleeting duration and on a nanometer scale, while the bulk solution remains near ambient temperature. These hotspots, combined with the presence of a low-concentration, environmentally benign radical precursor (such as hydrogen peroxide or even just trace dissolved oxygen activated by sonication), generate short-lived, highly reactive oxygen-centered radicals (e.g., •OH). These radicals attack the SWCNT sidewall at a rate that is orders of magnitude faster than thermal chemical reactions. The key to control and scalability is that the defect density is not governed by a slow, uncontrolled chemical equilibrium, but by the precise, tunable physical parameter of **sonication power and duration**. By adjusting these parameters, one can deterministically create the specific defect configurations (such as N- or S-defects) that act as quantum emitters, effectively converting the nanotube from a semiconducting channel into a luminescent quantum dot-like emitter.

This method is rapid because cavitation effects are instantaneous on the microsecond scale, allowing full functionalization in minutes rather than hours or days. It is mild because the bulk process occurs at room temperature and pressure, avoiding the thermal degradation of nanotubes and the need for extreme conditions. It is scalable because the process is continuous-flow compatible; dispersions or slurries can be passed through ultrasonic reactors, enabling industrial throughput. It avoids hazardous reagents by using water as the primary solvent and minimal, non-chlorinated, biodegradable radical precursors. Finally, the mechanism allows for real-time monitoring via in-situ photoluminescence spectroscopy, as the emergence and growth of near-infrared peaks directly correlate with the formation of quantum emitters, providing immediate feedback on process efficacy and enabling quality control during production.
