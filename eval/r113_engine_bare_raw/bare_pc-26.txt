My hypothesis is that the most efficient route to chiral indole-fused polycyclic frameworks relies on the formation of a covalent, chiral ammonium–imine intermediate or a highly organized, non-covalent hydrogen-bonded complex between a bifunctional organocatalyst (such as a chiral secondary amine bearing a hydrogen-bond donor group) and the indole substrate, which acts as the electrophile. In this model, the indole nitrogen is activated—either through iminium formation with an aldehyde or via hydrogen-bonding to a catalyst’s amide/thiourea moiety—creating a rigid, organized transition state that aligns the indole’s C2 position (the most nucleophilic site) with a dipolarophile, such as an enantioenriched aza-allylic anion or a Michael acceptor. The key mechanistic feature is the chiral catalyst’s ability to shield one face of the activated indole while simultaneously directing the nucleophilic attack through a six-membered cyclic transition state that minimizes steric clash and maximizes stabilizing hydrogen bonds. To exploit this, one should design bifunctional catalysts where the chiral backbone spatially constrains the reactive partners, ensuring that the nucleophile approaches exclusively from the less hindered quadrant of the indole plane. This precise steric control allows for high enantioselectivity during the key C–C bond-forming step, leading to the rapid assembly of the polycyclic core with defined absolute configuration, which can then be subjected to subsequent functionalization to access drug-like natural product analogs.
