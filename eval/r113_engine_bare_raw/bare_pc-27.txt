My hypothesis for optimizing the Biginelli reaction to balance high yields with minimal environmental impact centers on the mechanism of the iminium ion formation and subsequent Knoevenagel condensation. Traditionally, strong Brønsted or Lewis acids are used to activate the carbonyl groups of the aldehyde and β-ketoester, facilitating nucleophilic attack by the urea. This step is often the rate-limiting one. To exploit this mechanism sustainably, we can replace harsh reagents with mild, recyclable green catalysts that selectively enhance nucleophilicity and electrophilicity under milder conditions.

Specifically, I propose utilizing heteropolyacids or solid-phase metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) as catalysts in green solvents such as water, ionic liquids, or bio-based solvents like ethyl acetate or vegetable oils. The mechanism can be exploited by tuning the acidity of the catalyst just enough to activate the carbonyl without causing side reactions, thereby allowing the reaction to proceed at lower temperatures. This reduces energy consumption and minimizes decomposition of sensitive reagents. Furthermore, using water as a solvent can accelerate the reaction for many substrates due to the hydrophobic effect, which enhances the aggregation of reactants, while being benign and cheap. By combining a selective, non-toxic solid catalyst with a green solvent, we maintain the mechanistic integrity of the reaction—ensuring efficient iminium ion formation and subsequent cyclization to the dihydropyrimidinone—while drastically reducing waste, toxic byproducts, and purification complexity, thus achieving comparable yields with a significantly reduced ecological footprint.
