The central hypothesis is that high-quality COF synthesis relies on decoupling the kinetics of nucleation and crystal growth from the chemical stability of the linkers through the precise tuning of thermodynamic activity via specific solvent additives. The mechanism involves using additives that act as reversible catalysts or hydrogen-bonding modifiers to lower the activation energy for the supramolecular pre-assembly of monomers into ordered stacks, while simultaneously raising the kinetic barrier for irreversible covalent bond formation until the supramolecular order is established. This prevents the rapid, disordered polymerization that typically traps defects and collapses pores during subsequent dehydration or heating.

To exploit this mechanism, one should employ a dual-additive strategy where one component promotes supramolecular stacking through non-covalent interactions (enhancing crystallinity) and another component stabilizes the reactive intermediates (enhancing stability). By slowing down the covalent bonding rate relative to the supramolecular ordering rate, the framework grows layer-by-layer with high structural integrity rather than forming amorphous aggregates. This controlled kinetic pathway allows the preservation of the intrinsic high surface area of the monomers, resulting in a material that maintains its porous architecture under operational stress, thereby simultaneously optimizing crystallinity, surface area, and stability for catalytic and energy storage applications.
