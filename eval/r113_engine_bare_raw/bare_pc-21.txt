The core mechanism for achieving uniform, durable deposition of zinc oxide nanoparticles on cotton lies in exploiting the chemical reactivity of surface hydroxyl groups on cellulose to form covalent or strong coordinate bonds with zinc ions, thereby anchoring the nanoparticles directly to the fiber structure rather than merely coating them superficially. To exploit this, the process must move beyond simple electrostatic adsorption or physical entrapment, which are prone to washing out, and instead utilize a two-step grafting strategy. First, the cotton fibers should be chemically pretreated to increase the density of reactive sites; for instance, activating the cellulose with a silane coupling agent or an aminated monomer can introduce functional groups that specifically chelate zinc ions. Second, an in situ synthesis approach should be employed where a zinc precursor solution, such as zinc nitrate or zinc acetate, is infiltrated into the fiber matrix followed by the addition of a precipitating agent like sodium hydroxide or ammonia. This localized precipitation forces the formation of zinc oxide nanocrystals directly at the active sites on the fiber surface. Because the nanoparticles nucleate and grow anchored to these chemical bridges, they resist detachment during laundering. Furthermore, controlling the reaction rate and precursor concentration ensures that nucleation occurs uniformly across the available surface area, preventing the large agglomerates typical of conventional coatings. This method preserves the structural integrity of the cellulose because the chemical modifications are surface-limited and the in situ growth does not subject the fiber bulk to harsh thermal or mechanical stresses, resulting in a textile that maintains its mechanical properties while gaining durable antimicrobial functionality through intimate interfacial bonding.
