The central hypothesis is that the high performance of nickel-iron layered double hydroxides (LDHs) for oxygen evolution is not primarily a result of the synthesized precursor but rather stems from the in-situ electrochemical reconstruction into a specific, metastable mixed-valence oxyhydroxide phase where the interlayer anions play a crucial role in stabilizing the active layer spacing and facilitating charge transfer. The instability and lack of reproducibility often observed in literature arise because standard synthesis methods do not account for this reconstruction, leading to uncontrolled morphologies and variable active site accessibility.

To exploit this mechanism, the strategy shifts from optimizing the initial hydroxide structure to engineering the "pre-reconstructed" state or, more effectively, designing the interlayer environment to stabilize the active oxyhydroxide phase under operating conditions. Specifically, incorporating non-coordinating or weakly coordinating anions that remain in the interlayer galleries even during the deintercalation-reconstruction process can lock the active phase in a high-energy, catalytically favorable configuration, preventing it from relaxing into a lower-activity crystalline oxide form. Furthermore, by applying a short, controlled potential window "activation" protocol immediately after synthesis to deliberately trigger and standardize the reconstruction, one can create a uniform active surface. This approach allows for the decoupling of synthesis variability from performance metrics, enabling true comparison of intrinsic catalytic activity by benchmarking against the reconstructed active phase rather than the precursor. Stabilization is achieved by selecting interlayer species that enhance structural integrity against dissolution and phase transformation at high overpotentials, thereby extending the operational lifetime of the catalyst.
