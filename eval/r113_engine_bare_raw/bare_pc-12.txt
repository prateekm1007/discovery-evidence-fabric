The central hypothesis is that the synthesis of indole-carbazole-based porous semiconductors can be decoupled from high-temperature, volatile organic solvent conditions by leveraging the unique solvation properties of ionic liquids to drive in situ self-assembly into a metastable, porous morphology during mild-temperature polymerization. Specifically, ionic liquids act not merely as inert green solvents, but as structured media that impose electrostatic and steric constraints on the growing conjugated chains. By selecting a cation with a steric bulk commensurate with the indole-carbazole repeat unit and an anion that forms specific weak non-covalent interactions (such as halogen bonding or π–π interactions) with the heterocyclic nitrogens, one can stabilize transient, ordered aggregates during polymerization. When the ionic liquid is subsequently removed under mild vacuum or gentle thermal conditions, these aggregates undergo rapid, controlled phase separation, freezing a high void fraction into the final material. This "template-free" self-assembly mechanism allows the porous architecture to form kinetically rather than thermodynamically, bypassing the need for high-energy processing. To exploit this mechanism, engineers should first map the interaction energy landscape between candidate ionic liquids and the monomer/polymer backbone using computational screening, focusing on Gibbs free energy of mixing and the stability of pre-polymerization aggregates. Experimentally, the polymerization should be conducted at low temperatures (below 80 °C) in the selected ionic liquid, followed by rapid solvent exchange into a non-solvent (like water or ethanol) to precipitate the polymer while preserving the porous network. This approach significantly reduces organic solvent usage because the ionic liquid can be recovered and recycled via simple anti-solvent washing or distillation, and it lowers energy consumption by eliminating high-temperature reflux steps. The photoelectric performance is maintained or even enhanced because the controlled self-assembly promotes high crystallinity and charge carrier mobility within the conjugated backbone, while the porosity reduces optical density, potentially improving light harvesting efficiency per unit mass without sacrificing conductivity.
