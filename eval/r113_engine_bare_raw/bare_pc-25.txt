The core hypothesis is that high activity and stability in acidic media for platinum-free electrocatalysts can be achieved by leveraging synergistic electronic modulation and structural heterointerfaces within a dual-phase transition metal sulfide composite, rather than relying on noble metals. Specifically, the mechanism involves coupling a highly active but less stable metal sulfide phase (such as MoS₂ or CoS₂) with a stable, conductive transition metal carbide or nitride phase (like Ni₃C or TiN). The conductive phase serves to enhance charge transfer and prevent acid dissolution of the active sulfide, while the sulfide phase provides the necessary d-band electron density for optimal H* adsorption energy. To exploit this, researchers should synthesize these composites using a low-cost, one-pot solvothermal or hydrothermal method that simultaneously reduces metal precursors and sulfides them in situ, ensuring intimate atomic contact between the phases without requiring high-temperature sintering. This approach creates a stable, conductive scaffold that anchors the active sites, allowing the catalyst to maintain a high density of accessible active sites and robust performance in acidic electrolytes by preventing the leaching of active metal components.
