The primary mechanism driving the efficiency gap in nucleic acid delivery is the endosomal escape bottleneck, where internalized cargo remains trapped in acidic vesicles and degraded by lysosomal enzymes before reaching the cytoplasm. Cationic lipids address this through the "proton sponge" effect and membrane destabilization, but they often lack the stability for precise tissue targeting, while polymers provide superior pharmacokinetic control and stability but frequently fail to breach the endosomal membrane. The hypothesis is that a synergistic hybrid architecture, specifically utilizing block copolymers with a polyethylene glycol (PEG) corona for stability and a cationic core linked to cationic lipids, will significantly enhance transfection by combining the stability of polymers with the potent endosomal rupture capability of lipids.

To exploit this, the engineering must focus on creating a "smart" hybrid nanoparticle that is pH-responsive. The structure should consist of a stable, neutral core for circulation (using PEG or zwitterionic polymers) that collapses or unfolds in the acidic environment of the endosome to expose cationic groups. These exposed groups should be covalently linked or tightly packed with cationic lipids that undergo a phase transition (e.g., hexagonal $H_{II}$ phase) at low pH, actively disrupting the endosomal membrane. By tuning the pKa of the polymer amines to the specific pH of the target endosomal stage (typically pH 5.0–5.5), the system can remain inert in the bloodstream (minimizing off-target toxicity and immune activation) while triggering rapid membrane permeabilization only upon internalization. This spatiotemporal control allows for high nucleic acid loading capacity due to the multivalent cationic core, while the PEG shell and neutral character in circulation maintain the safety profile required for clinical use by reducing non-specific interactions with plasma proteins and the reticuloendothelial system.
