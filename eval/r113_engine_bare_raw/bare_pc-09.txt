The central hypothesis is that successful selective C-C bond formation between inert carbon centers relies on a concerted dual-catalytic cycle where the lifetime and spin-state of the open-shell radical intermediate are kinetically matched to the organonickel intermediate, rather than simply maximizing the steady-state concentration of radicals.

To exploit this, researchers must move beyond generic photoredox activation toward designing specific radical stabilization pathways. The mechanism likely involves a "radical clock" scenario where the photoredox catalyst generates a transient radical via single-electron transfer, but this radical must be trapped by the nickel catalyst faster than it can undergo decomposition, rearrangement, or non-selective coupling. 

The key to stabilizing these intermediates under mild conditions is leveraging the nickel catalyst as both a radical trap and a redox mediator. Specifically, the nickel species should undergo rapid radical addition to form a Ni(III) or Ni(I) intermediate, which then facilitates oxidative or reductive elimination to close the cycle. To ensure selectivity, the redox potential of the photoredox catalyst must be tuned so that it selectively activates the less nucleophilic or less reactive partner, while the nickel ligand environment is designed to lower the activation barrier for the specific C-C bond forming step. 

In practice, this means exploiting substrate-specific steric and electronic effects to pre-organize the radical and nickel catalyst for selective trapping. For example, using chiral or bulky ligands on nickel can create a chiral pocket that stabilizes the radical-nickel complex through non-covalent interactions, effectively increasing the radical's effective lifetime within the coordination sphere while excluding competing pathways. This approach transforms the challenge of radical instability into a feature, where the radical's short lifetime is compensated for by the ultra-fast trapping event facilitated by the optimized nickel complex, thereby enabling late-stage functionalization with high chemo- and regioselectivity.
