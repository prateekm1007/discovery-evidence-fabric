The central hypothesis posits that performance decline and resistance rise under accelerated voltage cycling are primarily driven by the mechanical destabilization of the cathode's ionomer and support structure, rather than solely the dissolution or coarsening of the platinum catalyst particles. Under cyclic potential stress, the cathode undergoes repeated expansion and contraction of its porous layer. This mechanical fatigue causes the delamination of the carbon support from the platinum nanoparticles and the cracking or thinning of the ionomer network, which are essential for proton and water management. Consequently, the electrochemically active surface area decreases, and the effective ion conductivity drops, leading to increased mass transport resistance and voltage loss.

To exploit this understanding for improved durability, the engineering approach must focus on enhancing the mechanical resilience and interfacial stability of the cathode architecture. This involves developing carbon supports that exhibit minimal volume changes upon hydration and dehydration, or utilizing composite supports that can accommodate stress without fracturing. Furthermore, the ionomer layer should be engineered with cross-linking agents or blended with more mechanically robust polymers to maintain structural integrity and prevent delamination from the catalyst layer during cyclic stress. By stabilizing the mechanical interface between the carbon, platinum, and ionomer, the electrode can resist the fatigue-induced degradation pathways that typically accelerate performance decline.
