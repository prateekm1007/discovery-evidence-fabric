The primary intermolecular forces governing the stable immobilization of uricase onto green-synthesized gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) in this context are **electrostatic interactions** and **hydrogen bonding**, with a secondary contribution from **van der Waals forces**.

Given that *Ganoderma lucidum*-synthesized AuNPs are typically capped with residual biomolecules (such as polyphenols, proteins, or carbohydrates from the mushroom extract) that impart a negative zeta potential to the nanoparticle surface, the immobilization of uricase (which generally has a net positive charge or a specific distribution of charged residues at its binding site) is driven by strong electrostatic attraction. Specifically, the positive patches on the uricase surface (often involving lysine and arginine residues) interact favorably with the negatively charged capping agents on the AuNP surface. Simultaneously, hydrogen bonds form between the hydroxyl and amine groups of the capping ligands and the polar amino acid side chains (serine, threonine, asparagine, etc.) on the uricase surface. While covalent bonding is not the primary mode in "green" synthesis without additional cross-linkers, the dense layer of green capping agents creates a stable non-covalent anchor.

To exploit this mechanism for enhanced stability, you should optimize the **pH of the immobilization buffer** to be near the isoelectric point (pI) mismatch that maximizes electrostatic attraction without inducing protein denaturation. Typically, immobilizing at a pH where the enzyme has a net positive charge (below its pI) and the nanoparticle surface is highly negative will strengthen the electrostatic binding. Furthermore, you can exploit the **steric protection** provided by the green capping layer; by ensuring the capping agent layer is sufficiently dense and stable, you create a physical barrier that limits the conformational flexibility of the uricase, thereby reducing denaturation from thermal or chemical stress. To further enhance stability, you could introduce mild cross-linking agents (like glutaraldehyde) that react with the amine groups on both the enzyme and the capping layer, effectively converting the transient electrostatic/hydrogen bond network into a more permanent covalent or quasi-covalent network, while retaining the protective steric effects of the nanoparticle scaffold.
