The central hypothesis is that interfacial degradation and recombination in perovskite solar cells are not independent phenomena, but rather coupled effects driven by the accumulation of interfacial defects and ionic instability, which subsequently generate localized strain that exacerbates mechanical failure. To exploit this mechanism, one must move beyond treating interfaces as simple passivation layers and instead engineer them as active, chemically graded buffers that chemically lock down ion migration while simultaneously accommodating lattice mismatches.

Specifically, this strategy involves designing interlayers with a tailored elastic modulus and composition gradient. By introducing a thin, flexible, and defect-poor transition layer between the perovskite and the charge transport materials, we can achieve three simultaneous goals. First, chemical compatibility at the interface reduces the density of trap states, thereby suppressing non-radiative recombination. Second, the graded mechanical properties act as a stress-release zone, mitigating the thermomechanical degradation caused by thermal expansion mismatch between the perovskite film and the underlying glass or metal electrodes. Third, and critically, this stable interfacial structure creates a higher energy barrier for bulk and interfacial ion migration, which prevents the formation of local chemical hotspots that typically trigger accelerated degradation. Therefore, the optimal engineering solution is a dual-interface strategy where the top and bottom interfaces are not merely optimized for energy alignment, but are specifically structured to decouple chemical stability from mechanical stability, allowing for robust charge extraction without the trade-off of mechanical fragility.
