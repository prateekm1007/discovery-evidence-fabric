Lithium loss in cathode precursors during thermal processing is primarily driven not just by simple volatilization, but by the reaction of surface lithium species with trace acidic impurities (such as CO2 or H2O) and the migration of lithium ions through the lattice to the surface where they become trapped or react, particularly at temperatures where the solid-phase stability limits are approached. The mechanism involves a coupled electrochemical and chemical diffusion process where lithium vacancies diffuse rapidly through the material, allowing surface lithium to react with the environment or sublime, rather than being a uniform bulk depletion. To exploit this understanding, one should control the thermal environment by using inert atmospheres with strictly regulated partial pressures of oxygen and water, and implement rapid thermal cycles that minimize the time the material spends in the temperature window where lithium diffusion is fastest but structural integrity is weakest. Furthermore, modifying the precursor's surface chemistry or adding passivation layers can block the exit pathways for lithium ions, effectively trapping them within the lattice by slowing down solid-state diffusion relative to the reaction rate, thereby preventing uncontrolled loss.
