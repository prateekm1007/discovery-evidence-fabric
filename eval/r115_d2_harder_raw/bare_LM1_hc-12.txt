The mechanism for tuning the morphology of hydrothermal ferberite between nanoplatelets and microspheres without dopants lies in the manipulation of the supersaturation gradient and the relative growth rates along the crystallographic axes, which are dictated by the solubility kinetics and the interfacial free energy of specific crystal facets. The paper’s insight into the growth mechanism suggests that the reaction environment—specifically the concentration of the iron precursor and the pH stability during the hydrothermal process—determines whether growth proceeds via a layer-by-layer deposition (favoring 2D nanoplatelets) or a radial aggregation of nanocrystallites (favoring 3D microspheres).

To exploit this for optimizing visible-light photocatalytic performance, one must identify which morphology maximizes the active surface area exposed to solar radiation while minimizing bulk recombination sites. Typically, nanoplatelets offer high surface-to-volume ratios and expose specific, catalytically active low-energy facets more readily, which enhances the separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs. However, if the platelets aggregate, their photocatalytic efficiency drops. Conversely, microspheres may provide better light scattering properties (increasing photon path length) but often suffer from higher internal recombination rates due to their bulkier nature.

Therefore, the hypothesis is that selectively tuning the hydrothermal conditions to favor the formation of **exfoliated, single-crystalline nanoplatelets** (rather than aggregated microspheres) will yield superior visible-light photocatalytic performance. This is achieved by controlling the reaction time and precursor concentration to maintain a moderate supersaturation level that promotes lateral growth over vertical stacking, ensuring the dominant exposure of the most active facets. The H2O2-assisted sunlight degradation in the paper serves as the test case: the morphology that facilitates the most efficient transfer of photogenerated holes to the H2O2 molecules (or radicals derived from it) on the surface will be the one with the highest specific surface area and optimal band-edge alignment, which is theoretically predicted to be the well-separated nanoplatelet structure.
