The hypothesis centers on the integration of conductive nanofillers, specifically graphene, into the polymer matrix to create a percolating network that dramatically increases the membrane's electrical conductivity without compromising its structural integrity or filtration selectivity. By blending graphene into the polysulfone precursor solution prior to phase inversion, the resulting composite membrane achieves a conductivity orders of magnitude higher than that of the pristine organic polymer. This enhancement allows the membrane itself to function as an electrode, enabling the application of low-voltage, in-situ electrical fields directly across the filtration interface. The mechanism for fouling mitigation exploits the induced electrical double layer and electrostatic repulsion generated by this field. As foulant particles, which typically carry a charge, approach the membrane surface, the applied electric field creates an energy barrier that repels them, thereby reducing the adhesion and deposition that lead to fouling. To exploit this, one must engineer the graphene loading to sit just above the percolation threshold, maximizing conductivity with minimal impact on pore structure, and then apply a controlled alternating or direct current during operation to actively sweep away or repel charged foulants in real-time, extending membrane life and reducing cleaning frequency.
