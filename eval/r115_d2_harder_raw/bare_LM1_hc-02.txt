The primary hypothesis is that the instability and efficiency losses in halide perovskites are driven by the formation of intrinsic point defects, particularly halide vacancies and interstitials, which act as non-radiative recombination centers and facilitate ion migration. This leads to photo-induced phase segregation and chemical degradation, such as the release of toxic iodine or the formation of dark copper iodide phases.

To exploit this, the material strategy involves multi-site compositional engineering using larger cations (such as rubidium or caesium) in combination with halide mixing. Specifically, incorporating small inorganic cations into the A-site lattice reduces the tolerance factor, which geometrically suppresses the migration pathways for halide ions and stabilizes the crystal structure against moisture and oxygen. Simultaneously, mixing bromide with iodide creates a localized strain gradient that pins the halide vacancies, preventing the formation of large perovskite domains that are susceptible to photo-induced migration. This dual approach of structural rigidity through cation mixing and defect passivation through anion management effectively blocks the ionic migration pathways, thereby inhibiting phase segregation and improving long-term operational stability while maintaining high power conversion efficiency by minimizing non-radiative losses.
