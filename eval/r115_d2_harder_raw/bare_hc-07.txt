The primary mechanism for performance decline and resistance rise in PEMFC cathodes under accelerated voltage cycling is the synergistic effect of platinum particle growth (coarsening/Ostwald ripening) and the redistribution of ionomer species. Specifically, the oxidative environment during voltage cycling causes the sintering and aggregation of Pt catalyst particles on the carbon support. As these particles grow, they attract and aggregate fluorine and sulfur species from the ionomer onto their surfaces. This accumulation of ionomer residues on enlarged Pt particles, combined with the loss of electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) due to coarsening, blocks access to active sites and increases transport resistance within the electrode.

To exploit this mechanism and improve long-term durability, electrode design should focus on enhancing the chemical stability of the catalyst-ionomer interface and inhibiting mass transport of platinum species. This can be achieved by developing catalysts with higher resistance to sintering, such as alloyed Pt-based catalysts or core-shell structures that stabilize the platinum surface against coarsening. Furthermore, the ionomer formulation should be modified to improve its chemical stability under oxidative conditions and reduce its tendency to migrate and aggregate on growing metal particles; this could involve using high-molecular-weight or chemically cross-linked ionomers that are less susceptible to decomposition and redistribution. By stabilizing both the catalyst particles and the ionomer morphology, one can mitigate the specific degradation pathways of particle growth and ionomer accumulation, thereby maintaining ECSA and low resistance over extended cycling.
