**Verdict:** The draft proposal is **flawed** because it misprices the duty cycle and relies on an unavailable or insufficient chemical specification to meet the failure mode. Specifically, raising the caustic concentration alone does not address the root cause of "hard-water scale" (inorganic mineral deposits), which is chemically resistant to alkalis. The proposal incorrectly assumes that "losing bite between acid descals" is fixed by higher alkali concentration rather than by addressing the scale or the acid descaling efficacy. Furthermore, while 0.8 mol/L NaOH is within the gasket rating, the failure mode is inorganic scale, which requires acid (or chelating agents) to dissolve, not base. Bases clean organic soils (fats/proteins) but do not remove calcium/magnesium carbonate scale. Therefore, the mitigation fails to address the actual failure and may worsen the situation by leaving the scale intact while only cleaning organic residue on top of it.

**Detailed Reasoning:**

1.  **Mismatch of Chemical Mechanism (Physical/Chemical Direction):** The failure is explicitly identified as "hard-water scale" (inorganic mineral deposition, primarily CaCO₃/Mg(OH)₂). Scale removal requires an **acidic** or chelating environment to dissolve the inorganic salts. The proposal suggests increasing the **caustic (basic)** concentration. While caustic CIP removes organic fouling (fats, proteins), it does **not** remove hard-water scale. In fact, high-pH environments can sometimes stabilize scale or prevent the acid from acting effectively if not properly managed. The draft inverts the chemical logic: you cannot dissolve inorganic scale with a stronger base.

2.  **Failure to Address "Losing Bite" Between Acid Descals:** The problem states the caustic CIP is "losing bite between acid descals." This implies the acid descal is currently insufficient or infrequent, allowing scale to build up and interfere with caustic performance. The draft keeps the acid descal frequency at "fortnightly" (every two weeks) but does not explain how this frequency is sufficient to prevent scale buildup if the system is failing. More critically, the draft relies on raising the caustic concentration to compensate for the loss of effectiveness. This is a misdiagnosis: the loss of "bite" is due to the scale layer shielding the plate surface from the caustic. Raising the caustic concentration will not penetrate the scale layer; only increasing the acid descal intensity or frequency (or using chelants) will remove the scale.

3.  **Constraint Check (Gasket Rating):** 
    - The constraint states gaskets are rated for up to **2.5 wt% NaOH at 75 °C**.
    - The proposal uses **0.8 mol/L NaOH**.
    - Conversion: 0.8 mol/L × 40 g/mol (molar mass of NaOH) = 32 g/L.
    - Assuming density ≈ 1 kg/L, this is approximately **3.2 wt%** NaOH.
    - **Violation:** 3.2 wt% exceeds the gasket rating of 2.5 wt%. Therefore, the proposal **violates the stated constraint** regarding gasket safety. Even if it were within the rating, the chemical logic is flawed.

4.  **Duty Cycle/Mispricing:** The draft proposes keeping the acid descal "fortnightly." If the scale is the primary issue, the duty cycle of the *acid* step is likely mispriced (too low frequency or insufficient concentration). The draft ignores the need to adjust the acid step and instead shifts load to the caustic step, which is chemically inappropriate for inorganic scale.

**Conclusion:** The draft violates the gasket concentration constraint (3.2 wt% > 2.5 wt%) and fails to address the root cause (inorganic scale) by using an ineffective chemical agent (increased base) for that specific failure mode. It should be rejected.
