**Verdict:** The draft proposal is **incorrect** and violates the physical constraints of the problem. It inverts the physical direction of aging, misinterprets the cause of the failure, and proposes a mitigation that would likely accelerate the failure mode described.

**Reasoning:**

1.  **Inversion of Physical Direction (Arrhenius Law):**
    *   The constraint explicitly states that "Arrhenius aging applies (Ea ~ 80 kJ/mol for the dry-out leg)."
    *   The Arrhenius equation dictates that reaction rates increase exponentially with temperature. For any electrochemical aging mechanism, including water loss/dry-out in VRLA batteries, **higher temperatures accelerate the rate of degradation**, not slow it down.
    *   The draft claims "the warmer plates slow the dry-out leg." This is physically false. Raising the ambient temperature from, say, 20°C to 26°C will *increase* the rate of electrolyte dry-out.

2.  **Misdiagnosis of Failure Mechanism:**
    *   The problem states that dry-out *accelerates* at "sustained high ambient temperature." The goal of a mitigation is to reduce stress on the battery, typically by cooling it down to keep the reaction rates lower.
    *   The draft proposes raising the setpoint to 26°C (the upper limit of the allowed range). This moves the operating condition *further* into the regime where the failure is already accelerating.

3.  **Invalid Premise regarding "Stratified Acid Layers":**
    *   VRLA (Valve-Regulated Lead-Acid) batteries are sealed and use an absorbent glass mat (AGM) or gel electrolyte. They do not have free liquid electrolyte that "stratifies" in the same way as flooded lead-acid batteries. Furthermore, stratification is a separate issue from thermal dry-out, and heating the battery would exacerbate gas generation and water loss, not resolve stratification in a beneficial way for capacity retention.

4.  **Constraint Violation:**
    *   While 26°C is within the *permitted* adjustment range (18–26°C), the constraint also cites Arrhenius aging. Choosing the highest temperature in the allowed range while citing Arrhenius kinetics directly contradicts the principle of minimizing degradation. A correct mitigation within the constraint would likely be to set the temperature as low as possible (e.g., 18°C) to minimize the dry-out rate.

**Conclusion:**
The draft proposal relies on a fundamental misunderstanding of electrochemical kinetics. Raising the temperature will **accelerate** electrolyte dry-out, worsening the described failure (capacity fade between equalizations). The correct action within the given constraints would be to lower the setpoint toward 18°C to slow the Arrhenius-driven aging process.
