**Mitigation Proposal: Fouling-Adjusted Heat Transfer Monitoring and Predictive Cleaning Trigger**

**Mechanism:**
Implement a software-based fault detection and diagnostic (FDD) loop that continuously calculates the *effective* overall heat transfer coefficient ($U_{eff}$) of the exchanger in real-time using only existing process variables. Instead of relying on a fixed temperature setpoint, the system monitors the *deviation* between the expected heat duty (based on mass flow and inlet/outlet temperature differences under clean conditions) and the actual heat duty. As fouling accumulates, $U_{eff}$ drops, causing the outlet temperature to rise for a given flow and load. The mitigation involves using this calculated performance degradation to trigger cleaning *before* the outlet temperature exceeds a critical limit, rather than waiting for a manual schedule or total failure.

**Implementation:**
1.  **Baseline Establishment:** During the initial "clean" state after a chemical cleaning, record the steady-state relationship between brine mass flow rate ($\dot{m}$, from the flow meter), brine inlet temperature ($T_{in}$, from thermocouple), and brine outlet temperature ($T_{out}$, from thermocouple). Calculate the baseline log-mean temperature difference ($\Delta T_{lm}$) and the associated baseline heat duty ($Q_{baseline} = \dot{m} \cdot c_p \cdot (T_{out} - T_{in})$). Store the corresponding baseline $U_{clean} = Q_{baseline} / (A \cdot \Delta T_{lm})$ (where $A$ is known surface area).
2.  **Real-Time Monitoring:** Create a continuous calculation routine that runs every minute:
    *   Read current $\dot{m}$, $T_{in}$, and $T_{out}$.
    *   Calculate current $\Delta T_{lm}$ (assuming shell-side temperature is stable or its variation is slow enough to be tracked via the brine-side energy balance).
    *   Calculate current heat duty $Q_{current} = \dot{m} \cdot c_p \cdot (T_{out} - T_{in})$.
    *   Calculate current effective U-value: $U_{eff} = Q_{current} / (A \cdot \Delta T_{lm})$.
3.  **Fouling Factor Estimation:** Compute the fouling resistance $R_f$ using the formula $R_f = \frac{1}{U_{eff}} - \frac{1}{U_{clean}}$. Track the trend of $R_f$ over time.
4.  **Action Trigger:** Set a threshold on $R_f$ (e.g., when $R_f$ exceeds 20% of its maximum allowable value, or when $U_{eff}$ drops by 10% from $U_{clean}$). When this threshold is crossed, automatically generate a "Cleaning Required" alarm and suggest scheduling a chemical cleaning. This prevents the outlet temperature from creeping up to unacceptable levels by ensuring cleaning occurs based on actual thermal performance degradation rather than calendar time.

**Measures Relied Upon:**
*   **Brine Mass Flow Rate ($\dot{m}$):** From the existing flow meter.
*   **Brine Inlet Temperature ($T_{in}$):** From the existing inlet thermocouple.
*   **Brine Outlet Temperature ($T_{out}$):** From the existing outlet thermocouple.
*   **Known Physical Constants:** Brine specific heat capacity ($c_p$) and exchanger heat transfer area ($A$) are process knowledge, not new sensors.
*   **Assumption of Quasi-Steady-State Shell-Side Conditions:** The method assumes that for the short-term monitoring intervals, the shell-side temperature profile is sufficiently stable or that its impact is dominated by the brine-side fouling trend. If shell-side conditions vary significantly, the baseline $U_{clean}$ might need periodic re-calibration during known clean periods, but no new hardware is added.

This approach uses *only* the existing instruments to derive a new performance metric (fouling resistance trend), enabling proactive maintenance and preventing the gradual rise in outlet temperature between cleanings.
